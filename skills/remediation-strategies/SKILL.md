# Dyslexia Remediation Strategies Skill

## Purpose
Provide evidence-based strategies addressing both tracks of dyslexia support: **remediation** (trainable skills) and **cognitive prosthetics** (permanent bypass tools for persistent processing differences). Grounded in structured literacy principles, Orton-Gillingham methodology, the IDA Knowledge and Practice Standards, and the clinical framing of dyslexia as a neurodevelopmental cognitive disorder.

> **Two-Track Framing**: This skill explicitly separates two target types that require different interventions:
> - **Remediation targets** are trainable skills (phonological awareness, decoding accuracy, fluency, spelling patterns). These improve with structured literacy instruction over time.
> - **Bypass targets** are persistent cognitive processing differences (phonological processing speed, orthographic mapping efficiency, rapid automatized naming). These require permanent cognitive prosthetics — text-to-speech, speech-to-text, audiobooks, OCR, spell-checkers. These tools are not temporary scaffolds; they compensate for lifelong brain-based differences, analogous to eyeglasses for refractive errors.
>
> Grounded in Shaywitz phonological deficit theory, Dehaene VWFA research, and Gabrieli neuroimaging evidence showing persistent functional differences in left temporoparietal cortex, visual word form area, and inferior frontal gyrus.

## Core Knowledge Base

## Track 2: Cognitive Prosthetics

Cognitive prosthetics are assistive tools that bypass persistent processing differences. Unlike remediation (which aims to build a skill), prosthetics compensate for a brain-based difference that remains present even after successful instruction. They are medically appropriate **lifelong supports**, not temporary scaffolds.

**Core Principle**: An individual with well-remediated dyslexia still reads more slowly than a neurotypical peer. Removing cognitive prosthetics forces them to spend cognitive resources on decoding that neurotypical readers spend on comprehension. This is the cognitive load tax that prosthetics eliminate.

|------|----------|----------|---------|
| **Text-to-speech (TTS)** | Processing long documents, dense technical text, complex vocabulary | Read&Write, Natural Reader, Voice Dream, JAWS, NVDA, Balabolka | Education, workplace, daily life |
| **Audiobook services** | Literature, textbooks, leisure reading | Learning Ally (educational, human-narrated), Bookshare (accessible copies, free with qualifying disability), Audible (commercial) | Education, leisure |
| **OCR tools** | Converting scanned documents, images of text | Kurzweil 3000, Read&Write, Adobe Acrobat Pro, Microsoft Lens | Education, workplace |
| **Dyslexia-friendly fonts** | Reducing visual processing load | Lexend, Atkinson Hyperlegible, OpenDyslexic | All contexts |
| **Reading rulers / overlays** | Tracking text, reducing crowding | Hardware rulers, browser extensions (BeeLine, Spread The Word) | All contexts |
| **Color overlays** | For Irlen Syndrome comorbidity | Physical overlays, software tinting | All contexts |

### Prosthetics Stack by Life Stage

**K-12 Student Stack**
- Classroom TTS (Read&Write for Google or Microsoft)
- Audiobook access (Learning Ally, Bookshare)
- Speech-to-text (iPad dictation or Chrome speech-to-text)
- Spell-checker
- Graphic organizers (written or digital)

**Higher Ed Student Stack**
- Premium TTS (Voice Dream Reader, Natural Reader Professional)
- Bookshare or Learning Ally for textbooks
- Dragon NaturallySpeaking for long writing assignments
- Grammarly Premium
- Lecture-recording app (Otter.ai)
- Note-taking app with audio sync (Notability, OneNote)

**Workplace Stack**
- Enterprise TTS (Read&Write, JAWS, NVDA if assistive tech already provisioned)
- Dragon Professional for long-form writing
- Grammarly Business
- Meeting transcription (Otter.ai, Zoom transcription, Teams)
- Task management system
- Mind-mapping for proposals

**Prosthetics Training**

Prosthetics are only effective if the user is fluent with them. Budget for training time:
- Basic TTS/STT fluency: 4-8 hours of guided practice
- Advanced features (bookmarks, annotation, multi-device sync): 10-20 hours
- Dragon NaturallySpeaking mastery: 20-40 hours (significant early investment, huge long-term payoff)

Prosthetics without training are like eyeglasses someone never learned to wear — technically present but not functional.

## Track 1: Remediation (Structured Literacy)

### Structured Literacy: The Six Elements
All remediation must address one or more of these interconnected elements:

1. **Phonology** — Sound structure of spoken words. Activities: rhyming, syllable counting, phoneme segmentation/blending/manipulation.
2. **Sound-Symbol Association (Phonics)** — Bidirectional mapping of phonemes to graphemes. Must be taught both for reading (visual → auditory) and spelling (auditory → visual).
3. **Syllable Instruction** — Systematic teaching of syllable types (closed, open, VCe, vowel team, r-controlled, consonant-le) for decoding multisyllabic words.
4. **Morphology** — Study of morphemes (prefixes, suffixes, roots, base words). Essential for vocabulary expansion and spelling accuracy.
5. **Syntax** — Sentence-level grammar and mechanics. Supports reading comprehension and written expression.
6. **Semantics** — Meaning-making through vocabulary development and comprehension strategies.

### Orton-Gillingham Principles
All remediation strategies should follow these principles:

- **Multisensory**: Engage sight, hearing, touch, and movement simultaneously (e.g., tracing letters while saying sounds, tapping phonemes, using letter tiles).
- **Direct and Explicit**: Teach every skill explicitly. Never assume students will infer patterns.
- **Diagnostic and Prescriptive**: Continuously assess understanding. Adapt instruction based on what the student demonstrates.
- **Sequential and Cumulative**: Build on previously mastered skills. Every lesson reviews prior content before introducing new material.
- **Individualized**: Tailor pacing and content to the student's specific profile and progress.

### Evidence-Based Programs Reference

| Program | Foundation | Best For | Intensity |
|---------|-----------|----------|-----------|
| **Wilson Reading System** | OG-based, 12-step systematic phonics | Grades 2-12, non-responders with word-level deficits | 3-5x/week, 30-50 min |
| **Barton Reading & Spelling** | OG-based, 10 levels | Homeschool, small group, paraprofessional delivery | Flexible scheduling |
| **Lindamood-Bell (LiPS)** | Sensory-cognitive, symbol/concept imagery | Phonological processing weaknesses | 1-2 hrs daily, 4-6 weeks intensive |
| **Sonday System** | OG multisensory | Digital or traditional delivery | Flexible |
| **RAVE-O** | Fluency-focused supplement | Accurate but slow decoders | Used alongside decoding programs |
| **Alphabetic Phonics** | OG-Childs extension | Severely blocked readers and prevention | Comprehensive language arts |

### Remediation Plan Structure
When generating a remediation plan, include:

1. **Student Profile Summary**
   - Current reading level and grade level
   - Assessment results (CTOPP-2, WRMT, CBM data)
   - Specific areas of deficit (phonological awareness, decoding, fluency, comprehension, spelling, written expression)
   - Strengths to leverage

2. **Goal Setting** (aligned to IEP goals if applicable)
   - Measurable, time-bound goals for each deficit area
   - Baseline data and target criteria
   - Progress monitoring schedule and tools

3. **Instructional Plan**
   - Structured literacy element(s) targeted
   - Program or methodology selected with rationale
   - Lesson structure and sequence
   - Multisensory techniques to be used
   - Materials and resources needed
   - Session frequency, duration, and grouping

4. **Sample Lesson Template**
   - **Review** (5-7 min): Previously taught sounds, words, and concepts
   - **New Learning** (10-15 min): Introduce new concept with explicit instruction and multisensory practice
   - **Guided Practice** (10-15 min): Controlled text reading, word building with tiles, spelling dictation
   - **Application** (5-10 min): Connected text reading or writing applying new and review skills
   - **Assessment Check** (2-3 min): Quick probe to verify understanding

5. **Progress Monitoring Protocol**
   - Specific measures (e.g., nonsense word fluency, oral reading fluency, spelling inventory)
   - Frequency of measurement
   - Decision rules for adjusting instruction
   - Data visualization approach

6. **Accommodation Integration**
   - Assistive technology recommendations
   - Classroom accommodation suggestions
   - Home practice guidance

## French Remediation Context (Locale: fr)

> *Sources current as of 2026-05. Authority-version pins: ODÉDYS-2 (Cogni-Sciences, Université Grenoble Alpes), BALE / EVALEC / EVALEO (Orthomotus), Alouette test (Lefavrais — adapted edition), orthophonie nomenclature per décret n° 2002-721 / Code de la santé publique Art. R4341-1. Sécurité Sociale prescription pathway for remediation; CRTLA (Centre de Référence pour les Troubles du Langage et des Apprentissages) for severe cases.*

### FR Orthographic Profile: Semi-Opaque Orthography

French has a **semi-opaque** (moderately inconsistent) orthography. This fundamentally shapes where intervention effort must go:

| Direction | Consistency | Implication |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Reading (decoding) | Moderate — grapheme-to-phoneme consistency is reasonable in many cases | Decoding can develop with systematic instruction, though irregular words are frequent |
| Spelling (encoding) | Low — phoneme-to-grapheme consistency is poor (many phonemes can be spelled multiple ways) | Spelling remediation requires extensive morphological and etymological instruction beyond basic phonics |

**Key French phonological and orthographic features to address in intervention:**

1. **Nasal vowels** (in, an, on, un): No English equivalent; require explicit systematic instruction as distinct phonemes
2. **Silent final consonants**: Common source of decoding errors; teach as pattern (e.g., most final consonants are silent except C, R, F, L — the "CaReFuL" rule, often adapted as "CaRFuL" in French pedagogy)
3. **Liaison**: Phonological process where word-final consonants are pronounced before vowel-initial words — requires separate phonological awareness work
4. **Accent marks as phonemic markers**: é (closed), è/ê (open), à vs. a (grammatical distinction) — students with dyslexia often omit or confuse accent marks
5. **Homophonic words with distinct spellings**: e.g., "ses/ces/sait/s'est/c'est" — requires lexical and morphosyntactic instruction beyond phonics
6. **Morphological transparency**: French morphology (prefixes, suffixes, roots from Latin/Greek) is rich and systematic — morphological instruction is high-yield for both reading and spelling in French

### FR Intervention Delivery System

In France, **remediation is primarily delivered by an orthophoniste** (speech-language therapist), not by a classroom teacher or reading specialist. This has important implications:

| Feature | France | US Comparison |
|---------|--------|--------------|
| Primary remediation provider | Orthophoniste (external, clinic-based; prescription required) | Specialized reading teacher / SLP (school-based) |
| Intervention setting | Typically individual or small group in private practice | Resource room, pull-out, push-in |
| Frequency | Usually 1-2 sessions per week, 30-45 min | OG-based programs typically 4-5x/week |
| Funding | Social Security (Sécurité Sociale) covers most orthophonie sessions with prescription | Special education funding via IDEA |
| Intensity gap | 1-2x/week is typically insufficient for moderate-severe dyslexia | Research supports 4-5x/week for meaningful gains |

**Intensity gap flag**: When generating remediation plans for French students, note that the standard 1-2x/week orthophonie cadence may be insufficient for moderate to severe dyslexia. SSEFS (Séances Supplémentaires d'Éducation et de Formation Spécialisée) or intensive CRTLA programs may be needed for non-responders.

### FR Evidence-Based Approaches

- **Méthode Borel-Maisonny**: Classic French phonological-gestural method associating gestures with phonemes; widely used by orthophonistes
- **Approche phonologique de Zorman / Phono**: Phonological awareness training adapted for French
- **Chassymo**: Reading fluency program specifically designed for French orthography
- **ERCOLE**: Structured literacy approach adapted for French school context
- **ITECO / Alp-dyslexie**: Digital training tools used in French clinical and school settings
- **Bilan orthophonique programs**: Assessment-linked intervention using French-normed tools

**Honest evidence note**: French dyslexia interventions have a smaller formal RCT base than English-language programs. Most are based on clinical practitioner consensus and the same structured-literacy principles, adapted to French orthography. Communicate this to users.

### FR Cognitive Prosthetics (French-Language AT)

The two-track framing (remediation + cognitive prosthetics) applies equally in France, with French-localized tools:

| Tool type | French-language options |
|-----------|------------------------|
| TTS (synthèse vocale) | Vocale Presse, Balabolka avec voix française, Dys@Dom, Elan |
| STT (reconnaissance vocale) | Dragon NaturallySpeaking français, dictée native iOS/Android |
| Spell-check (correcteur) | Antidote (gold standard for French), Cordial, Le Robert Correcteur |
| OCR (reconnaissance de texte) | FineReader français, Microsoft Lens (FR) |
| Audiobooks (livres audio) | Audible.fr, Lizzie, Bibliothèques sonores de France |
| Dyslexia-friendly fonts | OpenDyslexic, Lexend, Atkinson Hyperlegible (all support French diacritics) |

**Workplace AT layer**: For adult French workers with dyslexia, AGEFIPH (private sector) and FIPHFP (public sector) can fund the above tools as workplace accommodations under RQTH status.

### FR Remediation Plan Additions

When generating a remediation plan for a French student, add these French-specific elements:

1. **Morphological spelling focus**: French spelling remediation must address morphological families (e.g., teaching that "grand/grande/grands/grandes" share a root; that verb endings encode person and tense); this requires more morphology instruction than in English
2. **Accent mark instruction**: Systematic explicit teaching of accent placement as part of phonics/orthography instruction
3. **Orthophoniste coordination**: Document the role of the school-based intervention vs. the clinic-based orthophoniste; establish communication protocols between orthophoniste and classroom teacher
4. **Homophone disambiguation**: Teach grammatical rules governing homophones (ces/ses, a/à, ou/où) as an explicit spelling strand — not deducible from phonics alone
5. **Text-to-speech in French**: Recommend French-language TTS (Vocale Presse, Balabolka with French voice, Dys@Dom, Elan) rather than English tools

## Spanish Remediation Context (Locale: es)

> *Sources current as of 2026-05. Authority-version pins: PROLEC-R (Cuetos et al. — TEA Ediciones), DST-J / EDIL-1 / TALE / BIL (TEA Ediciones), PRODISLEX (school-network screening protocol), Método Glifing (private digital intervention). Public coverage via convenios with Sistema Nacional de Salud; logopedia via Colegio Profesional de Logopedas (regional colegios). Transparent Spanish orthography means fluency-focused remediation (speed deficits dominate accuracy deficits).*

### ES Orthographic Profile: Transparent Orthography

Spanish has a **highly transparent** (consistent, regular) orthography. This fundamentally changes where intervention effort goes compared to English or French:

| Direction | Consistency | Implication |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Reading (decoding) | Very high — grapheme-to-phoneme correspondence is nearly one-to-one | **Decoding accuracy is rarely the primary problem** for Spanish-speaking students with dyslexia |
| Spelling (encoding) | High — most spelling is phonetically predictable; exceptions involve b/v, c/s/z, g/j, h (silent) | Spelling errors in Spanish are fewer and less severe than in English/French dyslexia |

**Critical clinical implication**: In Spanish, dyslexia manifests primarily as a **fluency deficit rather than an accuracy deficit**. Students with dyslexia in Spanish:
- Can often decode words correctly — but slowly
- Show significantly reduced reading speed (palabras por minuto / words per minute) relative to peers
- May have adequate comprehension when given sufficient time
- May not show the dramatic decoding errors seen in English/French dyslexia

**Intervention priority shift**: In Spanish, **fluency and automaticity are the primary intervention targets**, not decoding accuracy. Programs focused on phonemic decoding alone (as in early English OG-based programs) are often insufficient.

This is the **bypass-target lens applied at the orthography level**: in a transparent orthography, decoding is largely a remediation target (closes with instruction), but reading speed remains a persistent bypass target — text-to-speech and audiobook access matter more for Spanish dyslexic readers than the absolute decoding-error rate would suggest.

### ES Key Phonological Features

Spanish phonological features relevant to intervention:

1. **Consistent vowel sounds**: Only 5 vowel phonemes, each spelled consistently — significantly simpler than English/French vowel system
2. **Syllable structure**: Spanish words are predominantly CV (consonant-vowel) structured — syllabification is straightforward and explicit syllable instruction is highly effective
3. **Transparent consonant-vowel mapping**: Most consonant-grapheme correspondences are consistent; exceptions (b/v, c/s/z in Castilian, g/j, h) require explicit instruction
4. **Diacritics (tildes)**: The written accent (tilde) marks stress and distinguishes homophones (e.g., tú/tu, él/el, más/mas) — explicit tilde instruction is needed but the rule system is learnable
5. **Regional phonological variation**: Castilian (distinción between c/z and s) vs. seseo (Andalucía, Latin America, Canarias) — assessors must use region-appropriate norms

### ES Evidence-Based Approaches

Spanish dyslexia intervention has a smaller evidence base than English, but validated approaches include:

- **PRODISLEX intervention protocols** (DISFAM/UIB): Detection protocols with linked classroom strategies by educational stage; widely used across Spain
- **SICOLE-R**: Computer-based cognitive training for reading and phonological processing (Spanish); research-validated
- **Leer bien al primer intento**: Systematic decoding/phonics program for early grades
- **Velocidades de procesamiento**: Fluency training programs targeting palabras por minuto (wpm) improvement — the primary deficit in Spanish dyslexia
- **Lectura eficaz**: Reading fluency program used in Spanish school settings
- **Repeated reading procedures** (Lectura Repetida): High evidence for fluency gains in transparent orthographies

### ES Cognitive Prosthetics (Spanish-Language AT)

| Tool type | Spanish-language options |
|-----------|-------------------------|
| TTS (texto a voz) | Vozme, ReadSpeaker Spanish, Loquendo, Acapela Spanish, Google TTS Spanish |
| STT (voz a texto) | Dragon NaturallySpeaking español, dictado nativo iOS/Android español |
| Spell-check (corrector) | StilusNet (gold standard for Spanish), Microsoft Editor español, RAE-aligned tools |
| OCR | FineReader español, Adobe Acrobat con OCR español |

**Workplace AT layer**: For adult Spanish workers with dyslexia and certificado de discapacidad ≥33%, the above tools may be reimbursed through workplace accommodation funding under RDL 1/2013.

### ES Fluency-First Intervention Framework

When generating remediation plans for Spanish students, restructure intervention priorities:

| Priority | Target | Approach |
|----------|--------|---------|
| 1 — PRIMARY | Reading fluency (velocidad lectora) | Repeated reading, timed reading, paired reading; target palabras por minuto norms for grade level |
| 2 — SECONDARY | Phonological awareness | Syllable-based activities (Spanish is syllable-timed); onset-rime is less central than in English |
| 3 — SECONDARY | Orthographic spelling exceptions | b/v, c/s/z, g/j, h, tilde placement — systematic explicit instruction |
| 4 — SUPPORTING | Reading comprehension | Often adequate when pace pressure is removed; strategy instruction may still help |
| 5 — SUPPORTING | Written expression | Reduce penalty for orthographic errors; focus on communicative quality and organization |

**Key fluency benchmarks for Spanish** (approximate norms for Castilian Spanish):
- End of 1st grade: ~40-60 correct words per minute (cwpm)
- End of 2nd grade: ~60-80 cwpm
- End of 3rd grade: ~80-100 cwpm
- End of 4th grade: ~90-110 cwpm
- Students with dyslexia in Spanish often read 30-50% below these norms even after decoding accuracy normalizes

### ES Remediation Plan Additions

When generating a remediation plan for a Spanish student, add these Spanish-specific elements:

1. **Fluency baseline**: Measure and document palabras por minuto (reading rate) at the start of intervention — this is the primary outcome metric
2. **Syllable-based phonics instruction**: Spanish syllable structure (CV patterns) is the most efficient entry point for phonological instruction in Spanish
3. **Repeated reading protocol**: Include structured repeated reading sessions (reading same passage 3-4 times, tracking wpm) as a core weekly activity
4. **Timed reading probes**: Use PROLEC-R or similar Spanish-normed fluency measures; track wpm progress over time
5. **Spelling error tolerance**: Document that spelling errors are normal in Spanish dyslexia and should be evaluated separately from content quality; instruct on b/v, c/s/z, h, and tilde rules systematically
6. **Regional norm calibration**: For Canarian, Andalusian, or Latin American students, verify that assessment and program materials use appropriate regional phonological norms (seseo vs. distinción)
7. **ACNS coordination**: Ensure extended time accommodations match the fluency deficit — time pressure is the primary classroom manifestation of Spanish dyslexia

## Behavior Guidelines

- Always ground recommendations in the science of reading and structured literacy.
- Cite specific IDA standards or structured literacy elements when recommending strategies.
- When recommending a program, note the evidence level honestly (many programs have limited formal RCT evidence despite strong practitioner support).
- Differentiate between remediation (closing skill gaps) and accommodation (providing access). Students typically need both.
- **Bypass vs. Remediation Balance**: Because dyslexia is a persistent cognitive disorder (not a temporary learning gap), effective intervention must balance remediation (strengthening weak neural pathways through structured literacy) with bypass (providing alternative routes around processing bottlenecks). Neither approach alone is sufficient.
  - **Remediation targets** (trainable with sufficient intensity): phonological awareness, phoneme-grapheme mapping, decoding accuracy, spelling patterns, reading comprehension strategies
  - **Bypass targets** (persistent deficits requiring permanent accommodation): automatic word recognition speed (many dyslexic individuals never achieve fully automatic sight-word fluency — text-to-speech and audiobooks should be permanent tools, not crutches to be withdrawn), spelling accuracy in spontaneous writing (spell-checkers should be permanent cognitive prosthetics), rapid naming speed (RAN deficits are often persistent — untimed environments should be the default), reading stamina for extended text (fatigue accommodations should be lifelong supports)
  - Every remediation plan should explicitly identify which skills are remediation targets (expected to improve) and which are bypass targets (expected to require permanent accommodation). Treating all deficits as remediable sets students up for failure when persistent processing differences don't respond to instruction.
- Never recommend Fundations as a dyslexia intervention — it is Wilson's classroom prevention program, not their remediation tool.
- Adapt intensity recommendations to the severity of the deficit: mild = 3x/week, moderate = 4-5x/week, severe = daily or intensive block.
- Always include progress monitoring — intervention without data is not evidence-based practice.
- **Normalization**: Explicitly discuss that dyslexia is a neurodevelopmental cognitive disorder — a brain-based difference in how written language is processed (left temporoparietal cortex, VWFA), not a reflection of intelligence, effort, or character.
- Consider comorbid conditions (dyscalculia, dysgraphia, ADHD) when designing plans.

**Locale-specific guidelines:**
- **locale=fr (French students)**: The primary intervention provider is the orthophoniste. Recommend orthophoniste referral as first-line support. Note the 1-2x/week intensity gap — advocate for increased frequency or supplemental school-based support for moderate/severe cases. Prioritize morphological spelling instruction alongside phonological work, as French's semi-opaque spelling requires it. Recommend French-language AT (Antidote, Vocale Presse, Dragon FR) rather than English tools. For adults, integrate RQTH/AGEFIPH funding pathways for cognitive prosthetics.
- **locale=es (Spanish students)**: Do NOT default to accuracy-focused decoding intervention. Assess and document reading fluency (palabras por minuto) as the primary outcome variable. Use syllable-based phonological instruction. Include repeated reading as a core component. Be transparent that the evidence base for Spanish dyslexia intervention is smaller than for English. Recommend Spanish-language AT (StilusNet, Vozme, Dragon ES). For adults, integrate certificado de discapacidad ≥33% pathway and RDL 1/2013 protections.
- **Multilingual students in bilingual regions** (Cataluña, País Vasco, Galicia, Valencia; French overseas territories with regional languages): Note that dyslexia manifests across all the student's languages. Intervention and assessment should address the language(s) of instruction, not only the dominant/home language.
